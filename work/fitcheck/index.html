ProblemFree returns are why people in India buy clothes online. You can't tell if something fits, so you order it and send it back. A quarter to a third of orders come back. Size is the biggest reason. Each one costs the retailer shipping both ways, handling, and often a garment it can't sell again. The chart that would prevent it sits on the page, unread.
DecisionBuild a calculator, not a guess. It compares the fit a garment was designed for with the fit you'd actually get. The chart is already exact, so arithmetic is the right tool here.
EvidenceTen installs. 70% were still using it a week later. Nobody uninstalled it. And it refuses to answer when the size chart is missing or unreadable — eight cases where it says so instead of guessing.
OutcomeIt caught a real sizing mistake of mine. One that had already cost a return and an exchange. Every verdict can be checked against the store's own chart.

FitCheck
Waist measurement 30 in

The label said 30. So did my waist.

I ordered Levi’s jeans in size 30. My waist is 30 inches. They arrived too loose.

I sent them back and exchanged them for a 28. That should not have been possible.

Then I read the size chart on the page I had just bought from. On that cut, a size 28 is made for a 30-inch waist. The label was never a measurement. It was a name.

The chart had been sitting on that page the whole time I was ordering the wrong thing. Three shipments, one out, one back, one out again, for a pair of jeans whose correct size was published two scrolls down.

Not long after, Myntra, AJIO, H&M and Westside were all running sales at once and I had a tab open on each. I shop for clothes on a laptop, because a bigger screen shows me more of what I am about to buy. That preference is the only reason I noticed.

Across four tabs, the same letter meant four different bodies. An M on one site was not the M on another. Myntra and AJIO were worse, because they are aggregators. Every brand inside them ships its own chart.

I am an XS in some places. An S in most. An M in a few. There is no version of me that is one size.

And every one of those pages already had the chart on it. The information was never missing. It just asked me to do arithmetic across four tabs, in my head, while a sale timer ran.

So the chart was never the problem. Trusting it was.

How it works

It reads the chart the shop already published, works out your fit from it, and can build your profile from something you already own.

Reads the pageEach of the three stores publishes its chart differently, so there is a small reader for each one. They all hand back the same set of numbers.
Does the mathsYour body against what the garment was actually cut for. Two inches of room means slim. Eight means oversized.
Uses what you ownOn a past order it knows the size you bought, and can build your profile from it. So you never have to measure yourself.

Non-goals, and why

Seven things, and none of them were turned down for being hard to build. The first one was the original idea for the whole product.

One retailerThe first version was a feature inside Myntra. I sketched it, then dropped it. You cannot ship inside Myntra’s page without being Myntra. And the problem is not inside any one shop. It is that four shops disagree about what an M is.
3D and try-onBoth chase accuracy. Every page already has a size chart, so accuracy was never the missing piece. Trust was. Promise a perfect fit and you only get to be wrong once.
Your measurementsMost people do not know their bust, waist and hip. That is where they quit the form. But everyone knows which shirt fits them.
WestsideIts chart is published as a picture. I could have fetched the numbers from a server, but I had set a rule that the extension never calls out to the network. I kept the rule and dropped the retailer.
The true inseamReal inseam scales with height, at about 0.45 of it. I use 0.3. Levi’s lists a 32-inch inseam for every waist from 28 to 40. The anatomically right answer would flag jeans as too short that are sold to tall women on purpose.
A guessWhen the chart is short a number it says it cannot tell. That happened six times in twenty-one attempts. A confident answer with nothing behind it costs more trust than no answer.
The extension itselfIt only runs on desktop, about 40% of fashion traffic here. It exists to test whether a fit verdict changes what people buy, on the one surface I can reach without permission. The version that reaches everyone runs on the retailer’s own page.

What it still cannot tell me

A working engine, one real case where it would have saved a return, and nowhere near enough users to know whether it holds.

Ten installsSeven still using it a week later, nobody uninstalled. Recorded by hand in a spreadsheet, because there is no telemetry in this. Ten is a signal, not a study.
Six refusalsIn twenty-one attempts, so almost one in three. Not crashes. The engine read those pages fine and the chart was short a measurement. Coverage is a bigger limit here than accuracy.
Two body blocksMale and female, because retailers organise their charts that way. That underserves anyone those two blocks do not describe. A measurements-first model is the next thing I would build.
The number I wantVerdict-to-purchase. Of the people shown a recommendation, how many buy that size. It is what a retailer would pay for. I cannot measure it from outside.
What I can measureThe override rate. How often someone is shown a size and picks a different one. The cleanest trust signal there is, and the one most fit tools never instrument.
