FitCheck

ProblemFree returns are why people in India buy clothes online. You can't tell if something fits, so you order it and send it back. A quarter to a third of orders come back. Size is the biggest reason. Each one costs the retailer shipping both ways, handling, and often a garment it can't sell again. The chart that would prevent it sits on the page, unread.
DecisionBuild a calculator, not a guess. It compares the fit a garment was designed for with the fit you'd actually get. The chart is already exact, so arithmetic is the right tool here.
EvidenceTen installs. 70% were still using it a week later. Nobody uninstalled it. And it refuses to answer when the size chart is missing or unreadable — eight cases where it says so instead of guessing.
OutcomeIt caught a real sizing mistake of mine. One that had already cost a return and an exchange. Every verdict can be checked against the store's own chart.

FitCheck
Waist measurement 30 in
Live on Myntra. It disagrees with your last purchase, and says why.
A size it turns down, with the axis that fails and where to go instead.
How it works, in three steps:
1. It reads the page. Each of the three stores publishes its size chart in a different way, so there is a small reader for each one. They all hand back the same set of numbers.
2. It does the maths. Your body against what the garment was actually cut for. Two inches of room means slim. Eight means oversized.
3. It looks at what you already own. On a past order it knows the size you bought, and can build your profile from it — so you never have to measure yourself.
The calls I made, and why:
• Exact, not estimated. The store already publishes the numbers, so the fit is worked out straight from them. A centimetre of drift here costs someone a return and a reshipment, so nothing about the answer is approximated.
• Nothing leaves your browser. Your measurements stay on your own machine. No account, no server, nothing sent anywhere.
• It tells you when it can't tell. If the chart is missing, incomplete or published as a picture, it says so rather than inventing an answer. Eight defined cases where it does that.

123 Logged Decisions · Zero Number Hallucinations

Savio

ProblemMoney apps tell you where your salary went. None answer the question you actually have. Can I afford this, right now? Will it break something I've already promised? So people guess. Guessing is how a month quietly goes wrong.
DecisionCode works out every number. The AI writes the sentence you actually read. Before it reaches you, every figure in that sentence is traced back to your real data. If one can't be traced, Savio tells you to check rather than showing it.
EvidenceIn testing the AI claimed 8 of 9 purchases were regrets. The real answer was 7 of 8. I could have added another checker on top. Instead I took the AI out of that job and let plain code do it. 123 decisions are written down, including the ones I changed my mind on.
OutcomeA live app that would rather stay quiet than show a number it can't stand behind. Trust built in, not promised in small print.

Savio
The home screen. One number, and what it is made of.
It answers, shows the maths, and names the rule it used.
Core Product Concept:
1. It picks its moment. Not a popup at checkout, when you have already decided. It steps in when your salary lands, when a windfall arrives, and a few days after you bought something. Quiet moments, when you can actually think.
2. The AI does the explaining, grounded in your own figures. Code produces the numbers. The AI turns them into something you would act on, and it can only draw on what is really yours: your commitments, your safety net, what is left this month. That is the harder half of the job. A correct number nobody reads changes nothing.
3. It quotes your own rule back to you. Not "this is over budget", but "over the ₹3,000 impulse-wait limit you set". A rule you wrote yourself is a rule you will accept.
PM Guardrails & Trust:
• Every number gets traced. The AI is good at explaining; it is not the place to source a figure. So each number it writes is checked back against your real data before you see it, and anything that can't be traced gets replaced with a note telling you to check.
• Your safety net is the floor. Savio flags anything that would eat into the cash you said you want to keep, or into what you have already promised elsewhere. Green, amber or red, with the reason.

Amazon Discovery Intelligence

ProblemA PM cannot read every review, so they skim. Skimming feels fine because you never find out what you missed. The complaint that mattered was in there, and nobody counted it.
DecisionThirteen steps, and AI runs at three of them. It reads the messy text and groups the complaints, which is what it is genuinely good at. Every number a PM then sees is worked out in code.
Evidence140 reviews go in. 136 survive the clean, and the 4 that don't are reported, not quietly dropped. Every claim in the digest links to the review behind it. Anything the evidence doesn't support gets flagged on the page.
OutcomeA week of reviews becomes a ranked list a PM can act on. Delivery and Tracking came top at 81.6, marked Must Have. You can see why, and what was left out.

This week’s ranking
Every group ranked, with the evidence count behind each one.
Ask it anything. Each claim footnotes the review it came from.
Core Product Concept:
1. It gathers the complaints. Play Store, App Store and Amazon listings. Play Store is the one that reliably delivers; the others chip in when they can.
2. Sample or live, never mixed. A switch at the top decides whether you are looking at a fixed test set or reviews pulled this week. The two never blend, so a demo can never be mistaken for real data.
3. Then it ranks. How many people hit it, how badly, how many sources agree, whether a recent version made it worse, how hard it looks to fix, and whether it is trending up or down. Formally: (reach × impact × confidence × version) / effort × trend.
PM Guardrails & Auditing:
• AI reads, code ranks. Sorting thousands of messy complaints into themes is exactly what a model is good at, so that is its job here. Turning those themes into a priority order is arithmetic, and arithmetic is what a PM has to defend in a roadmap meeting.
• Nothing disappears quietly. If a review is dropped during the clean, the digest says how many went and why. 140 in, 136 kept.
• Every claim is traceable. Ask the digest a question and each sentence in the answer footnotes the review it came from.

62% Arrive from Google · Similarweb + SEMrush, Apr 2026

Reddit: a waypoint, not a destination

ProblemAround 62% of Reddit’s visitors arrive from Google, land on one thread, get their answer and leave. Reddit spends its welcome effort on a homepage those visitors never see.
DecisionPut the welcome on the thread page itself. A slim strip naming the community and showing it is active, in place of the app-install wall that currently covers the answer and that Google penalises.
EvidenceSimilarweb and SEMrush agree on the shape: 62% from Google, 63% on a phone, and the most common next step is leaving for another site. Alongside five interviews, a 15-person survey, and a Reddit thread with 48,000 upvotes where users describe the workaround themselves.
OutcomeThree fixes, one for each place the journey breaks, sequenced cheapest first. And a line not to cross: if Google traffic falls faster than it already is, stop. That traffic is the business.

3-Sided Market Strategy (Viewers · Creators · Advertisers)

YouTube's Shadow R&D Lab: How Modded Apps Shape Strategy

ProblemAnalytics only show what users do inside your app's rules — they miss what power users want badly enough to install third-party mods to fix.
DecisionRead community mods as a free R&D lab, then filter each signal through a three-sided market (viewers, creators, advertisers) to adopt, re-architect, or suppress.
EvidenceSponsor-skipping mods re-built into AI "Jump Ahead" heatmaps (saving creator revenue); playback controls moved to Premium; Shorts toggle suppressed to protect reach.
OutcomeA clear strategy for turning power-user workarounds into roadmap calls — without destroying the business model that pays creators.
