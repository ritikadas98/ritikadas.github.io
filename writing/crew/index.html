One Trip at a Time

Product case study

On CREW, Swiggy's travel concierge.

Problem
CREW’s best work is a person travelling with you, and those people are the hardest thing it has to hire.
Decision
Build a plan that watches itself, so a person is spent only where judgement is needed.
Outcome
More trips, without hiring at the same pace and without the service thinning out.

The Product: What CREW Does That No One Else Does

You have fifty-five minutes to catch your connecting flight, in an airport you have never been to, and it leaves from a different terminal.

You did not have to find that out. You were told before you landed, along with which train to take and how long it runs.

Nobody asked for that.

Any travel app can sell you the flight. Almost none will tell you about a terminal you have not reached yet — and the reason is not that the others never thought of it.

The Customer: Who This Is For, and Who It Is Not

Across the exchanges CREW publishes, nobody asks the price. No cheaper option, no second choice, no comparison. A recommendation arrives and the answer is book it.

Money is only half of that. The other half is somebody who has decided that choosing is the expensive part. What they are buying is not a better rate. It is one fewer decision on a day that already has too many.

Which makes the entrance worth a look. Ignore online rates, it says, and then sets its own against them — one of four price arguments before the app asks for ₹999. A comparison is an invitation to check, and checking is the habit that everything further in is built to remove.

That tension is the whole segmentation question, so it is worth naming both sides of it.

Worth most to. Expertise does not travel with you. Someone who knows Bombay street by street lands in Lisbon knowing nothing. The stakes are highest where the ground is new — a first long-haul trip, a holiday a year in the planning, a honeymoon — because one wrong call on those is not recoverable.

Worth least to. On the thirtieth flight through the same airport you already know where the lounge is, and you have your own visa routine, hotel and seat. For that traveller CREW is a nicer booking engine — and a nicer booking engine is exactly the product whose price you check.

Both of them fly. Only one of them is buying what CREW actually sells.

The Moat: Why a Competitor Cannot Copy It

The reason is not the data.

To send that airport message you need the flight number, the connecting flight, and both terminals. Every large booking platform holds all of it, for far more travellers, and none of them has ever sent anyone a message about a gate.

Three things have to be true at the same time.

  1. You need the trip.
  2. You need people who will stay with a customer through a day.
  3. You need a relationship that makes spending an hour on one traveller reasonable.

A booking platform has the first and cannot afford the second, because adding a service team breaks a business built on volume. A travel agent has the second without live data. A general-purpose assistant has neither the trip nor a reason to speak first.

That is a real position to hold, and very few companies are standing in it.

The Economics: Why Every Standalone Concierge Ended Up Inside Something Else

A concierge service standing on its own has three economic problems at the same time. The ones that came before died of the first two, long before the third became their limit.

Winning the customer. Customer acquisition cost is what it takes to win one customer — the advertising, the search, the brand-building. For a concierge that means finding people who travel expensively and will hand a holiday over, then persuading them one at a time. The purchase is seasonal and rare, so the payback period, which is how long that customer takes to earn the cost back, runs long.

Keeping them. Somebody travels a few times a year. A relationship that goes quiet for five months has to be rebuilt every time it comes back, and rebuilding is another cost.

It ends the same way every time. Ten Lifestyle ended up inside private banks. Velocity Black was bought by Capital One. American Express never sold its concierge separately at all. None of them lasted as a subscription somebody bought on its own.

CREW does not have to solve either one, and not because it is cleverer. Swiggy paid to acquire these customers years ago, over food and groceries, and it already knows from its own order history who spends. The relationship never goes quiet, because Instamart and Dineout keep it warm between trips. The acquisition cost of a CREW member is close to nothing, and the retention was bought by a different product entirely.

Serving them. The third cost is cost to serve: the human minutes one trip consumes. Nothing about sitting inside Swiggy inverts that one, because it does not scale with the size of the company. It scales with the number of trips.

And what comes in. Two revenue lines are confirmed. A subscription, which is the ₹999 the entrance asks for, and commission on what gets booked — the hotels, the villas, the experiences. The second is the one that scales with the trip rather than with the member, which is also why the entrance can afford to argue about rates: it is arguing about inventory CREW earns a margin on. A third is possible and I cannot confirm it. The co-branded card means the spend runs over Swiggy plastic, and whether any of that interchange comes back to CREW is not visible from outside.

Put the two sides together and you get the number the whole model turns on. Contribution margin is what is left of the revenue on a trip once the cost of serving that trip is taken out. Acquisition and retention were paid for by a different product, so the human minutes are very nearly the whole of that cost.

Which is why it is the one the rest of this piece is about. A plan that watches itself, the three tiers, the answer written down once — every one of them is an attack on the only cost CREW did not inherit an answer to.

Distribution: How It Reaches People Without Going Mass

CREW is hard to find on purpose. It is not a tile inside the Swiggy app the way Instamart and Dineout are, because it was never meant to be for everyone.

But Swiggy already has an invitation, and since March it has two. One BLCK is invite-only and carries a travel benefit. The co-branded HDFC card now splits into variants, the BLCK version at the top: a one lakh a month income floor, and extra cashback on travel.

That is the shape every concierge that survived eventually took, for the reason above: a gate somebody else already paid to build.

Swiggy owns both halves already. The card is the gate, the membership is the invitation, and one accompanied trip is what a member feels before committing a whole holiday to it.

The Constraint: Cost to Serve

There is a question underneath all of it. How does CREW grow without hiring thousands of these people, or becoming the thing it was built not to be?

Swiggy built its business on physical dispatch, where a rider is interchangeable. One in Indiranagar can deliver in Koramangala and the routing works out the same. That is what let the fleet scale.

The people who do this job are not interchangeable. They need hard knowledge — visa rules, how long a connecting flight really needs, what an airport is like at six in the morning — and the softer kind, which is harder to hire for: staying calm, reading a mood, knowing when not to speak.

That bar exists because the stakes do. A wrong burger is a refund and an apology. A missed visa clause or a missed connecting flight is somebody's one holiday of the year.

The thing that makes CREW hard to copy is the same thing that makes it hard to grow.

These people are rare. That is the moat, and it is also the ceiling.

Anything that scarce has to be rationed, and CREW rations it sensibly. It comes with a booking big enough to pay for it.

So the best proof of what CREW is worth arrives only after you have already trusted it with a trip. You find out whether you can hand over a trip by handing one over.

Trip planning raises that stake. CREW can take all of it.

The Proposal: A Plan That Watches Itself

Here is the feature I would build next, and it is not on the app today.

It stands on trip planning. That is the part of CREW that takes a traveller end to end — the flight out, the flight home, a day-by-day plan, and hotels chosen to fit it — and works through the changes they ask for along the way. The result is a trip held as one record, not a folder of separate bookings.

Trip planning does what a traveller asks. This does the part nobody thought to ask for.

A plan has a few parts that move on their own. The airport gate. The departure time. Not enough time for the connecting flight. Rain over an outdoor afternoon. Nobody checks them until it is too late.

Here is how it would work:

  1. The plan is a record. Every leg and every day has a time against it.
  2. The app watches the moving parts against it, all day, unasked.
  3. When one breaks the plan, the app finds the fix and writes the message.
  4. The concierge assistant checks it and sends it. In seconds, not in an afternoon.
  5. The traveller hears it from CREW first.

CREW's product pages show this moment as something an assistant does when a traveller asks. This is the same moment without the asking, and on every trip rather than the few where it came up.

It asks for nothing CREW does not already have. The plan is a record inside trip planning. The airport gate, the terminal and the delay are in the booking. The forecast is free. Even the tone is written, in the cards CREW publishes. This is assembly, not invention, which is exactly why it is the next thing to build and not the fifth.

You cannot move someone's Tuesday afternoon unless you know what was in their Tuesday afternoon. Now the app knows.

The Build: What to Do, and in What Order

The aim is to spend those rare people only where a person is genuinely needed. The messages sort into three tiers.

  1. Most are not judgement calls. They are the plan-watching messages above — structured data checked against a record the app already holds — and they can go out without anyone touching them.
  2. Above those sit the ones that change a plan or spend money. The system finds the alternative and writes the message. An assistant reads it and approves. The person moves from producing the work to signing it off.
  3. Everything else goes to someone who decides.

The second change is smaller and worth more. Requests arrive as one message and get worked as one job. A couple wants a day at a vineyard outside the city — and inside that sentence are seven questions. Is it open that day. What time. What to wear. Is there lunch. How do they get there. Who books the car. And can they get onto the tasting that is always full.

Six have answers already. One does not. Today all seven wait for the same person.

Then the third, and it compounds. When an assistant solves the one with no answer, it is written down against that place — what worked, and who to call when the obvious number does not.

The booking was never the output. The output was a fact nobody had recorded.

Done well, the traveller experiences it as someone paying attention. Underneath, it was arithmetic.

Some of this stays human for good. A masseuse at a villa in Goa is not an API, and an assistant sitting on hold for a supplier who will not pick up is an expensive message-relayer. Nobody has automated being the person who is answerable once something has gone wrong.

Measurement: How You Would Know It Worked

Two numbers to watch, and one test that would prove me wrong.

  • The let-go rate. The share of trips where the customer never had to hold on. Never checked a confirmation, never chased a reply, never queried a bill that was supposed to be settled. One number that restates the whole argument.
  • Cost to serve. The human minutes one trip consumes. The first number can be bought with headcount, so it needs this one beside it. If the let-go rate climbs while the minutes fall, the service is running at software margins. If it climbs while the minutes stay flat, CREW has bought quality it cannot afford to keep.
  • The test that would prove me wrong. Compare the members who lean on the service against those who only book and handle the rest themselves. If both come back at the same rate, the accompaniment was never the advantage, and the honest move is to unbundle it.

What Is Worth Protecting

Almost every travel product competes on the same two things. Price, or inventory. Cheaper, or more of it. CREW competes on attention, which is harder to buy and much harder to fake.

That is why the small moments keep turning out to be the best evidence. A message about a terminal you have not reached yet. A day rewritten before the rain arrives. A masseuse at a villa in Goa, arranged by somebody who picked up the phone. And now a whole trip held in one place, so the next of those messages has something to be about.

None of those is a feature in the usual sense. They are all the same thing. Somebody was paying attention on your behalf, and you did not have to ask.

Attention is the scarce thing here, not headcount. Every minute an assistant spends typing out a gate change is a minute not spent on the supplier who will not pick up the phone. Give the gate change to the app and the person is still there, on the call only a person can make. And the vineyard that would not answer in June is a phone number in the file by August.

Get that right and the ceiling moves. The same people cover more trips. The service does not thin out as it grows. And someone who has never flown this route still lands knowing which train to take.

That is a rare thing to have built, and rarer still inside a company that grew on the opposite instinct — speed, volume, riders who are interchangeable by design. It is worth protecting.
