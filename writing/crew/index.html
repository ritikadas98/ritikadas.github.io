One Trip at a Time

Product case study

On CREW, Swiggy's travel concierge.

Problem
CREW’s best work is a person travelling with you, and those people are the hardest thing it has to hire.
Decision
Automate the messages that need no judgement. Keep people for the ones that do.
Outcome
More trips, without hiring at the same pace and without the service thinning out.

The Product: What CREW Does That Almost No One Does

You have fifty-five minutes to catch your connecting flight, in an airport you have never been to, and it leaves from a different terminal.

You did not have to find that out. You were told before you landed, along with which train to take and how long it runs.

Nobody asked for that.

Any travel app can sell you the flight. Almost none will tell you about a terminal you have not reached yet. It is not because nobody else thought of it.

The Customer: Who It Is For

CREW says who it is for: affluent urban consumers who have money but no time. The second half of that is the real definition, and it is not about wealth. In the exchanges CREW publishes, nobody asks the price. A recommendation arrives and the answer is book it. This is not a product for somebody pricing one trip across five tabs. It is for somebody who would rather pay for the experience and hand over the headache. Money is only half of that. The other half is deciding that choosing is the expensive part.

Worth most to the first long-haul. Expertise does not travel with you. Someone who knows Bengaluru street by street lands in Lisbon knowing nothing. The stakes are highest where the ground is new — a first long-haul trip, a holiday a year in the planning, a honeymoon — because one wrong call on those is not recoverable.

Worth least to the thirtieth flight. On the thirtieth flight through the same airport you already know where the lounge is, and you have your own visa routine, hotel and seat. For that traveller CREW is a nicer booking engine — and a nicer booking engine is exactly the product whose price you check. There is still something in it for them. There is just less, and this is the traveller who checks the price.

The Moat: Why a Competitor Cannot Copy It

The reason is not the data.

To send that airport message you need the flight number, the connecting flight, and both terminals. Every large booking platform holds all of it, for far more travellers.

Plenty of them send alerts. An airline app will tell you its own gate moved, and a trip organiser will put the change on your phone. What none of them sends is the next sentence: your connection is on another carrier, from another terminal, and here is the train that gets you there in eight minutes. They message about a flight. Nobody messages about the trip.

Three things have to be true at the same time.

  • You need the trip.
  • You need people who will stay with a customer through a day.
  • You need a relationship worth spending an hour of a person’s time on.

A booking platform has the first and cannot afford the second, because adding a service team breaks a business built on volume. A travel agent has the second without live data. A general-purpose assistant has the trip — your boarding pass is sitting in your inbox — and a lock screen to speak from. What it does not have is anybody answerable when the plan breaks.

The Economics: Why Concierges End Up Inside Something Else

A concierge on its own has three economic problems at once. The concierges that came before CREW ran aground on the first two, long before the third one mattered.

  1. Winning the customer. Customer acquisition cost is what it takes to win one: for a concierge, finding people who travel expensively and will hand a holiday over to a service, one at a time. The purchase is rare, so the payback period — how long they take to earn that back — runs long.
  2. Keeping them. Somebody travels a few times a year, and a relationship that goes quiet for five months has to be rebuilt. Rebuilding is another cost.

That is what happened to them. Ten Lifestyle ended up inside private banks, Velocity Black inside Capital One. American Express never sold its concierge separately at all.

CREW inherits an answer to both, and not because it is cleverer. Swiggy paid to acquire these customers years ago over food and groceries, and knows from its own order history who spends. And the relationship that stays warm is Swiggy’s, not CREW’s. Instamart and Dineout keep the customer in Swiggy’s life every week, whether or not they are travelling. So when CREW invites them, it is not a stranger writing.

The third is different. Cost to serve is the human minutes one trip consumes. Sitting inside Swiggy does not change this one. It grows with the number of trips, not with the size of the company.

And what comes in. One line is confirmed: the ₹999 the app asks for up front, which buys six months. Skift reports that the subscription is not the only revenue, and CREW advertises no convenience fee on flights while passing hotel savings to members. So we can assume the earning happens upstream, in a share of what gets booked, taken from the supplier rather than the traveller.

That leaves one number for everything else to turn on. Contribution margin is a trip’s revenue minus the cost of serving it — and since acquisition and retention were paid elsewhere, the human minutes are nearly all of that cost.

Which is why the rest of this piece is about it. Everything proposed below attacks the one cost CREW still has to solve for itself.

Distribution: How It Reaches People Without Going Mass

Order food on Swiggy and, while the timer runs, a card appears offering six months of CREW and ₹1,000 off a first hotel. Tap it and you leave for a different app, a different brand and a membership to activate.

Swiggy will sell you CREW. CREW does not live there.

The difference matters more than it sounds. Instamart and Dineout are places you go inside Swiggy. CREW is an advertisement that takes you out of it. Those became tiles. This one became a door.

So why keep it outside, when the traffic is right there?

One answer is the customer. A ₹999 travel service does not sit well beside tonight’s dinner, and a concierge conversation does not fit a screen built for tracking a delivery rider.

The better answer is what CREW is being sold as. A separate app with its own name, its own brand and an activation code is a membership, and a membership is a thing you decide to join. That decision is most of what CREW is selling. You cannot ask somebody to make it inside a food order.

CREW is now on both app stores, announced in public, and the invitation is a card on a delivery-tracking screen. Access is still limited to paid subscribers and invited travellers, so the door is not open to everyone.

The Constraint: Cost to Serve

There is a question underneath all of it. How does CREW grow without hiring thousands of these people, or becoming the thing it was built not to be?

Swiggy built its business on physical dispatch, where a rider is interchangeable. One in Indiranagar can deliver in Koramangala and the routing works out the same, which is what let the fleet scale.

The people who do this job are not interchangeable. They need hard knowledge — visa rules, how long a connecting flight really needs, what an airport is like at six in the morning — and the softer kind, which is harder to hire for: staying calm, reading a mood, knowing when not to speak.

That bar exists because the stakes do. A wrong burger is a refund and an apology. A missed visa clause or a missed connecting flight is somebody's one holiday of the year.

These people are rare. That is the moat, and it is also the ceiling.

And CREW is not being built for a few hundred members. Skift, which interviewed the chief executive of Swiggy’s food business, reports a target of fifteen to twenty million consumers. Put the two facts beside each other and the problem is the whole business: a service whose best work is one person’s attention, aimed at a number of people no amount of hiring reaches.

None of this is visible only from outside. The head of CREW has asked the same question in public: “could we deliver the exact same experience, just as well, for the ten-thousandth person as we did for the tenth?” That is this ceiling, named from inside the product.

CREW has pulled two levers on this already.

  • It narrowed. CREW used to book restaurant tables, renew Aadhaar cards, source gifts, arrange birthday parties, find locksmiths and send out the laundry. It does none of that now. Travel was forty to fifty per cent of everything people asked for, and travel is all CREW does today. There is nothing left to narrow.
  • It partnered. CREW now lets a member tap into local destination experts: a guide in Hoi An who has been doing it for eighteen years, a Madrid native who guides in London. These are local partners rather than staff, which is the cheaper way to do it, because nobody is paid between trips. It is the right call and not a workaround: no software knows what somebody who lives there knows. But capacity still arrives one person at a time.

Both work. Neither gets cheaper as CREW grows.

CREW also cut prices, with rooms ten to fifteen per cent under other platforms. Worth having, but not an answer to this. Every booking platform negotiates rates, and a discount does not buy back a single concierge minute.

Which leaves an awkward proof. You find out whether you can hand over a trip by handing one over.

The Proposal: What I Would Build Next

CREW proved that people will pay for somebody paying attention on their behalf, and it proved it the expensive way, with people. You cannot automate a job before you know what the job is.

So here is a third lever, and it is not on the app today.

What CREW now does

CREW plans the trip, not only the bookings. It plans the itinerary. It books the hotels and the flights. It arranges cabs, activities and visas. It makes the restaurant reservations. Then, once the trip is booked, on-trip assist stays with the traveller for the whole of it, around the clock.

CREW describes this as staying with you “before the trip, through the trip, and when the plan inevitably needs a new plan.”

What it does not do

On-trip assist is where the message at the top of this piece came from. Somebody at CREW saw that the terminal had changed and wrote before the traveller landed. That already happens, and it is the product working.

What it is not is systematic. Most of what CREW does begins with a request. The app opens on a request button, and the traveller asks. The unprompted message arrives when a concierge is attending to that trip.

CREW says its concierges are proactive, and that AI supports them. That is a person anticipating, with software helping, and every outlet covering CREW reports it the same way round: a human is responsible for the request. MediaNama asks whether the software can make a booking or fix an itinerary without a person. A person can only anticipate on the trips they are looking at, and nobody is looking at every trip, all day, on every leg.

A few parts of a plan move on their own. The gate. The departure time. A connecting flight gone too tight. The cab that no longer meets it. Rain over an activity booked outdoors. Nobody checks them until it is too late.

Here is how it would work:

  1. The plan is a record. Every leg and every day has a time against it.
  2. The app watches the moving parts against it, all day, unasked.
  3. When one breaks the plan, the app finds the fix and writes the message, and a person checks whatever needs checking.
  4. The traveller hears it from CREW first.

Finding the fix is harder than finding the problem. Spotting that a connecting flight no longer works is a comparison. Deciding what to put in its place is a booking, with fares, seats and somebody’s preferences in it. The first tier starts where it does because that half is only detection.

This needs one thing, and it is narrower than it sounds. The record plainly exists: you cannot tell somebody their terminal has changed without holding their terminal. The question is whether anything reads it when nobody has asked. That I cannot see from outside. If something already does, the rest is assembly — the gate, the terminal and the delay are in the booking, the forecast is free, and the tone is already written in the cards CREW publishes. If nothing does, building that watcher is the first task, and it is worth doing on its own.

You cannot move someone’s Tuesday afternoon unless you know what was in their Tuesday afternoon. The app knows. What it does not do is look.

The Build: What to Do, and in What Order

The aim is to use those rare people only where judgement is needed. The messages sort into three tiers.

  1. Most are not judgement calls. They are facts the source itself asserts: the airline says the gate has moved, the airline says the flight is late. Relaying those is not a decision, and they can go out without anyone touching them.
  2. Next are the ones the app works out for itself: that the connecting flight is now too tight, that the afternoon needs rewriting, that the cab no longer meets the plane. Those are inferences. The app finds the alternative and drafts the message, and a concierge reads it and approves, rather than writing it.
  3. Everything else goes to someone who decides.

The risk is a wrong message. Travel data is messy, and an alert about a connecting flight that was never at risk costs more than saying nothing. It spends the trust the service runs on.

That is why the line between the first two tiers sits where it does. A fact can go out on its own. An inference waits for a person.

The next change is smaller and worth more. A request arrives as one sentence and gets worked as one job. A couple wants a day at a vineyard outside the city. Inside that sentence sit seven questions. Is it open. What time. What to wear. Is there lunch. How do they get there. Who books the car. And can they get onto the tasting that is always full.

Six have answers already. One does not. Today all seven wait for the same person.

And it compounds. When a concierge solves the one with no answer, it is written down against that place: what worked, and who to call when the obvious number does not.

The booking was never the output. The output was a fact nobody had recorded.

Some of this should stay human. A masseuse at a villa in Goa is not an API. Nobody has automated being the person who is answerable once something has gone wrong.

Measurement: How You Would Know It Worked

Four numbers to watch, and one test that would prove me wrong.

  • The let-go rate. The share of trips where the customer never had to hold on. Never checked a confirmation, never chased a reply, never queried a bill that was supposed to be settled.
  • Cost to serve. The human minutes one trip consumes. The first number can be bought with headcount, so it needs this one beside it. If the let-go rate climbs while the minutes fall, the service is running at software margins. If it climbs while the minutes stay flat, CREW has bought quality it cannot afford to keep.
  • Reuse of the file. The share of requests answered from a fact already recorded, rather than worked out from scratch. This is the compounding one, made countable. If the file is never reused, the seventh question was not worth writing down.
  • Wrong-message rate. The share of auto-sent messages a traveller or a concierge later contradicts. This is the guardrail on the first tier. A service that cries wolf has spent the trust it was meant to protect.
  • The test that would prove me wrong. Compare the members who lean on the service against those who only book and handle the rest themselves. If both come back at the same rate, the accompaniment was never the advantage, and the honest move is to unbundle it.

What Is Worth Protecting

Almost every travel product competes on the same two things. Price, or inventory. Cheaper, or more of it. CREW competes on attention, which is harder to buy and much harder to fake.

The terminal message. The afternoon rewritten before the rain arrives. The masseuse at a villa in Goa. None of it is a feature in the usual sense. It is one thing: somebody paying attention on your behalf, without being asked.

Attention is the scarce thing here, and you cannot hire your way to more of it. Every minute a concierge spends typing out a gate change is a minute not spent on the supplier who will not pick up the phone. Give the gate change to the app and the person is still there, on the call only a person can make. That call is what the minutes are freed for. CREW brought a guide in Hoi An into its network, at the moment its own people are the thing it can least afford more of. The gate change is what you automate so the concierge is free for the call. And the vineyard that would not answer in June is a phone number in the file by August.

Get that right and the ceiling moves. The same people cover more trips. The service does not thin out as it grows. And someone who has never flown this route still lands knowing which train to take.

That is a rare thing to have built, and rarer still inside a company that grew on the opposite instinct — speed, volume, riders who are interchangeable by design. It is worth protecting.
